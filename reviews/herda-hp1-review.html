Hands-on: what it's like to use

Size is the headline. The HP1 measures 99 × 41 × 27 mm, about the footprint of a car key fob that grew up. In hand it feels like a small remote, not a radio. It rides in a jeans pocket without the bulge you get from the Radtel P8 or OMIZN M9, and the included neck strap makes sense here: you can wear it and forget it's there. For kids, older relatives, or anyone who won't carry a "real" radio, this is the size that actually gets carried.

Sound is the surprise. Herda rates the speaker at 2W, and you hear it. Voices come through clear and loud, enough to hear across a room or over road noise without holding it to your ear. On radios this small the speaker is usually the first thing that gets cut. Not here.

Delay: I measured about 1 to 2 seconds between pressing PTT and the other HP1 hearing me. That's in line with the other PoC radios I've timed (the Radtel P8 was the same). Press, pause a beat, then talk, and you won't clip the start of your sentence.

Screen and setup: There's a small screen, enough to see status and which channel or group you're on. The two units came ready to talk to each other with no programming; Herda pre-installs the SIM and sets them up out of the box.

The catch is groups. There's no public group directory. You talk to the radios in your own group and that's it: no browsing for open channels or meeting other users the way you can on Zello-style apps. For a family or a work crew that's exactly what you want. For a hobbyist who wants to find other people to talk to, it's a dead end.

What it costs to own

The first year of service is included. After that, renewal is $25 per radio per year, optional, with no monthly bill. That's a fair price on its own, but it's about two and a half times what Radtel (about $10) and TIDRADIO ($9.99) charge to renew. For a pair the difference is about $30 a year; for a 10-radio crew it's about $150 a year.

Herda sells the HP1 direct from its own store in packs only, since you need at least two radios to talk. As of November 23, 2026: $129.99 for a 2-pack (about $65 a radio), $189.99 for 3, $249.99 for 4 and $369.99 for 6. Bigger orders are quoted. It isn't sold on Amazon. Over three years a pair costs about $230 with renewals, against about $162 for a Radtel P8 2-pack, so the HP1 is the pricier radio to keep running. If your crew works long shifts, look at the HP2: same size, but its battery swaps out and it comes with a second battery and a charging dock. If you want a lower price and the cheaper renewal, the Radtel P8 renews for about $10 a year instead of $25.

Who should buy it

  • Parents who want a screen-light way to reach a kid across town without a smartphone
  • Families checking in on an older relative who won't carry a full-size radio
  • Small teams (events, retail, property) who want a radio that's always on them
  • Anyone who has tried a PoC radio and found it too bulky to carry every day

Who should skip it

  • Hobbyists who want public channels and strangers to talk to
  • Anyone who needs to talk with no cell signal (look at the TIDRADIO TD-M2 Pro with FRS backup)
  • Heavy all-day talkers who can't charge mid-shift (the HP2's swappable battery is a better fit)
  • Buyers who want the cheapest radio to run for three years

Verdict

The HP1 earns 4.5 out of 5 because it nails the two things that decide whether a radio actually gets used: it's small enough to carry everywhere, and it's loud enough to hear when you do. Delay is normal for PoC, setup is nothing, and year one of service is in the box. Know the trade-offs going in: closed groups only, a $25 yearly renewal that's higher than rivals, and you buy direct from Herda rather than Amazon. For families and small teams who want a radio people will actually keep on them, it's one of the easiest recommendations on the bench.
